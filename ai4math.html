01 / RESEARCH QUESTION

From an intuition to a testable question

The initial idea is to treat mathematical subfields as “interpolation points,” use the number and difficulty of problems recently solved with AI and the extent of AI contribution as observations, and build a dynamic surrogate model to anticipate the next breakthrough. We retain this idea while defining the underlying space, events, and prediction target precisely.

Using only evidence available by time t, can we predict whether a subfield will have at least one verified AI-assisted resolution of an open problem within the next 180 days more accurately than simple baselines such as “historically active fields remain active”?

Define yi,t,H = 1 if and only if field i has at least one event meeting preregistered criteria in (t, t+H]. The default forecast horizon is H = 180 days, with robustness checks at 90 and 365 days. This is a binary event prediction for each field: several fields may have breakthroughs, so their probabilities need not sum to 1. Asking which field will produce the very next event across all of mathematics would require a separate competing-risks formulation. These targets should be evaluated separately.

Fields are not Euclidean coordinates

MSC codes identify categories; differences between codes are not mathematical distances. Use fine-grained MSC topics as nodes and build a weighted graph from co-classification, citations, and documented shared methods available before the cutoff. Then compare graph kernels and embedding-based models.

Evaluate forecasting and optimization separately

Passive historical observation defines a forecasting task. A budget-constrained sequential decision problem arises when we can select directions, allocate computing or human verification effort, and observe subsequent feedback. Derivative-free optimization (DFO) and Bayesian optimization are possible later research directions; they do not automatically provide convergence guarantees for the forecasting model.

Project summary. We propose an auditable event registry, temporal evaluation that prevents information leakage, and a separate framework for allocating research budgets. The released baseline uses synthetic data only; no predictive validity or research priority ranking is claimed.

An auditable event registry comes before field scores

Each record should include stable problem_id and event_id values; the problem statement and evidence that it was publicly open before the research began; MSC labels; result type; first public date; the earliest availability of independent verification; AI system and role; human involvement; original sources and proof links; verification method; the rationale for difficulty labels; reviewers; and revision history. Deduplicate preprints, journal versions, and press coverage of the same result. Store subsequent improvements on the same problem as linked, distinct events.

  • Strict primary endpoint: resolution of a previously public open problem, confirmed by independent expert review or reproducible formal checking. Formal verification still requires checking that the formal statement matches the original problem and that its assumptions and axioms are appropriate.
  • Separate secondary endpoints: improved bounds, algorithm discovery, conjecture generation, formalization of known theorems, and competition performance. These may provide predictive features, but they do not enter the strict primary-endpoint count.
  • Unverified / disputed / withdrawn: retain evidence and status changes; exclude unverified events from the primary endpoint. A missing record does not establish an absence of progress. Document coverage and missingness.

Two domain annotators should review each event independently, with disagreements and adjudication recorded. Difficulty should use predefined categories and uncertainty ranges, rather than relying only on how long a problem remained open. AI contribution weights are measurement conventions to test, not numerical facts supplied by a paper.

03 / MODEL DESIGN

Beyond cumulative progress: time and structure

1. Retain cumulative scores; measure change within a window

Fi(t) = Σe: τₑ ≤ t aie qₑ wₑ dₑ
vi(t; Δ) = [Fi(t) − Fi(t−Δ)] / Δ

Here τₑ is the earliest availability of verification evidence; aie is the fractional assignment across fields, summing to 1 for each event; qₑ is a binary indicator of primary-endpoint eligibility; wₑ is the AI contribution weight; and dₑ is difficulty. We retain the initial proposal’s illustrative weights of 1 / 0.5 / 0.2 for independent completion / collaboration / substantial assistance. Unverified events are excluded through qₑ = 0. Real-data analysis must compare equal weighting, alternative contribution weights, difficulty categories, and results without difficulty weighting.

A cumulative score describes history, not the probability of the next breakthrough. Windowed velocity is also affected by field size, research effort, publication delays, and publicity. Reliable measures of effort or active problems could be modeled as exposure. Without them, these factors remain unidentified, and greater coverage should not be interpreted as greater ease of discovery.

2. A reproducible graph-smoothing baseline

ŝ = arg mins ‖s − v‖² + λ sᵀLs = (I + λL)−1v,   L = D − A

For a symmetric graph with nonnegative weights, the Laplacian L is positive semidefinite, so the solution is unique for λ ≥ 0. Setting λ = 0 recovers the original velocities; increasing λ brings connected fields’ scores closer together. This encodes the hypothesis that progress in related fields may be correlated. Edges are not causal evidence that breakthroughs propagate. The baseline produces smoothed activity scores, not probabilities or prediction intervals.

3. Compare forecasting models when sufficient data are available

Start with baselines based on overall event prevalence, historical field frequencies, recent windowed velocity, and regularized logistic regression without graph structure. Then compare discrete-time survival or count models with graph-based alternatives. One candidate is a space–time kernel K((i,t),(j,t′)) = [exp(−βL)]ij ktime(t,t′), coupled with a binary or count likelihood. See Kondor & Lafferty (2002) for graph diffusion kernels. Graph relationships, embeddings, parameters, and probability calibration must all use training-period information only.

Partial pooling across fields can reduce extreme estimates from sparse events. If too few events are available, report insufficient evidence rather than fitting an unnecessarily complex graph neural network. Surrogate-model variance does not automatically provide reliable predictive uncertainty; probability calibration and interval coverage require evaluation on separate time periods.

4. From forecasting to research allocation

If future work records the costs and outcomes of computational experiments or human verification, we can study acquisition strategies based on expected information gain or verification benefit per unit cost, while retaining an exploration budget. Actions then change the observation distribution, so this task needs evaluation separate from passive forecasting. Mathematical research is not an arbitrarily queryable smooth black box. Applying existing DFO theory requires reexamining assumptions about local geometry, noise, stationarity, and budgets.

05 / HOW WE WOULD TEST IT

Success requires more than a compelling ranking

  1. Register the protocol and build the dataset

    Freeze the problem definition, labeling rules, field granularity, sources, and verification criteria. Preserve public and verification dates, and maintain traceable dataset versions that support corrections. Establish a reproducible collection process before deciding whether enough events exist for statistical forecasting.

  2. Use rolling temporal backtests

    At each historical cutoff, train only on information available at that time and predict the next 180 days. Training-label observation windows must end before the cutoff. Censor or exclude test samples without a full 180-day follow-up. Prevent future information from entering graph edges, feature scaling, parameter selection, calibration, or difficulty labels.

  3. Evaluate probabilities and rankings

    Use Brier score, log loss, and reliability diagrams as primary probability evaluations, comparing against an overall-prevalence baseline. For rare events, also report PR-AUC, event prevalence, and sample size. Treat top-k hit rate as a secondary metric with k fixed in advance. Estimate uncertainty using time-block resampling or nonoverlapping windows; overlapping forecast windows are not independent samples.

  4. Design ablations that can refute the hypothesis

    Compare no graph, shuffled graphs, historical-frequency-only models, alternative windows, and different contribution weights. Test the effects of excluding highly publicized laboratories, changing label granularity, and varying verification delays. If graph models do not consistently outperform simple baselines, we cannot claim that mathematical knowledge structure improves predictability.

  5. Evaluate prospectively before building a live service

    Freeze forecasts on a fixed schedule, publish timestamps, and score them after their outcome windows close. A later pipeline could discover candidate papers automatically and update records after human review. This page currently provides no automated collection, continuous updates, or real-world probability service.

Completed work and next steps

Completed: a research question and draft labeling protocol, review of representative primary sources, a graph-smoothing implementation using the Python standard library, synthetic events, and an interactive demonstration. Still to do: systematic real-event collection and independent dual review, historical knowledge-graph construction, model training, temporal backtesting, probability calibration, and prospective validation. No real-world claim is made about which mathematical subfield will have the next breakthrough.
